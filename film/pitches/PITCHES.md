# Pitches — Milestone 1

Three concepts for a 5–10 minute short. Each is built around one clear turn that a short can land without needing a feature's worth of setup.

---

## 1. RETURN WINDOW

**Logline:** A woman tries to return an unworn wedding dress one day after the 30-day window closes, and a by-the-book customer-service clerk has to decide how much a policy is worth.

**Genre / tone:** Deadpan dramedy. Dry and awkward at the top, quietly devastating by the end. Think fluorescent light, long takes, small gestures.

**Treatment:** Late afternoon, a near-empty department-store returns counter. MAYA (30s) slides a garment bag across: one wedding dress, tags on, receipt attached, 31 days old. DEV (20s), the clerk, recites policy with the calm of someone who has done it a thousand times. Maya doesn't argue so much as negotiate: store credit? a partial refund? a manager? Each request opens a crack in her composure, and each refusal gets harder for Dev to deliver. A ringing desk phone, a register that won't let him override, a manager who never comes. Neither says what happened, but we piece it together from what Maya won't say. In the final minute Dev does something small and against the rules: he keys in yesterday's date. The refund prints. Maya leaves without the bag's hanger, and Dev hangs the dress on the rack behind him, alone with it under the hum of the lights.

**Why it works as a short:** One location, two actors, one prop that does all the storytelling. The whole film is a single negotiation with rising stakes and a clean payoff, which fits ten minutes exactly. **This is the cheapest to shoot**: a counter, a dress, and a day.

## 2. THE QUIET CAR

**Logline:** On the last train of the night, a commuter who ignores the "Quiet Car" sign discovers the rule is being enforced by something that is listening.

**Genre / tone:** Contained horror. Slow-burn dread built on sound design and silence rather than gore.

**Treatment:** NOOR (40s), exhausted and taking a work call, boards the nearly empty last train and sits in the quiet car. The three other passengers (a student, an old man with a newspaper, a nurse in scrubs) glare, then go very still. The lights dip each time Noor speaks. When the student's phone buzzes, the lights cut out for a second, and when they return the student's seat is empty except for the phone, still vibrating. Now Noor understands and so do we: every sound is a risk. The remaining passengers communicate by gesture, by notes on a newspaper margin, by holding breath as the train rattles through tunnels. The doors won't open until the next stop, eleven minutes away. The film plays the stretch in near real time, as a sneeze, a dropped coin, and a ringtone become life-or-death. Noor makes it to the platform. As the doors close behind her, she exhales, and an automated voice says, *"Thank you for riding quietly."*

**Why it works as a short:** A single high-concept rule, established in two minutes and then exploited. Horror shorts travel well at festivals and online. The cost is moderate: one train-car set or a borrowed carriage, four actors, and heavy reliance on sound (which is cheap) instead of effects.

---

## 3. LOW TIDE

**Logline:** A father and his teenage daughter have forty minutes of low tide to find the time capsule her late mother buried on the beach, before the sea takes the only map back.

**Genre / tone:** Family drama with a ticking clock. Warm, windswept, bittersweet, with some laughs between the two leads.

**Treatment:** Grey morning on a wide northern beach. THEO (50s) and his daughter IRIS (16) haven't really spoken since the funeral. Iris has a hand-drawn map from her mother's notebook: paces from a rock, a line toward a lighthouse, an X that is only reachable at the year's lowest tide. Theo thinks it's a waste of time and Iris thinks he never knew her mother at all. They dig in the wrong place, argue, dig again, and each failed hole draws out a memory: Theo remembers the day the map was drawn, and Iris learns he was there. The water starts coming back. With the sea at their ankles they hit a rusted biscuit tin. Inside there is no grand message, just two letters, one addressed to each of them. They read them on the dunes, separately and then side by side, while the tide covers the holes they dug.

**Why it works as a short:** A literal deadline gives the film momentum, and the found object is a simple, emotional payoff. Two actors, one exterior location, natural light. It's cheap but cinematic, and the most emotionally accessible of the three.
