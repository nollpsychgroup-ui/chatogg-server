# Pitches — Milestone 1

Three concepts for a 5–10 minute short. Each is built around one clear turn that a short can land without needing a feature's worth of setup.

---

## 1. RETURN WINDOW

**Logline:** A woman tries to return an unworn wedding dress one day after the 30-day window closes. The clerk eight minutes from his closing count, with one warning already on file, has to decide what a policy is worth.

**Genre / tone:** Deadpan dramedy. Dry and awkward at the top, quietly devastating by the end. Think fluorescent light, long takes, small gestures.

**Treatment:** It's 5:52 p.m. at a near-empty department-store returns counter. DEV (20s) is counting down to his 6:00 drawer count. A laminated note taped to his register reads *NO OVERRIDES. THIS MEANS YOU, DEV. — K.* He already has one write-up and can't afford a second. MAYA (30s) slides a garment bag across: one wedding dress, tags on, 31 days old. The receipt is in two names, hers and a "J." A hospital visitor band is still on her wrist. Dev recites policy, and Maya negotiates in three rungs, each costing her more. **First**, store credit: she'd have to come back here and pick something out, and she can't. **Second**, a manager: Dev pages, the phone rings out, and he admits K. left at five. **Third**, she stops asking for the refund. "Whatever it's worth to you. Half. Less." Along the way she says "we were going to—", stops, and corrects herself to "I." We never learn what happened to J., only that it wasn't a jilting. The clock reads 5:59. Dev looks at the note, then keys in yesterday's date and K.'s override code. The full refund prints. Maya can't speak, nods once, and goes. Dev hangs the dress on the rack behind him and straightens the skirt. The clock flips to 6:00, the lights hum, and we hold.

**Why it works as a short:** It needs one location, two actors, and one prop that does all the storytelling. The film is a single negotiation that climbs three clear rungs to a small act that costs the man who does it. It fits ten minutes exactly and is **the cheapest to shoot**: a counter, a dress, a wall clock, and a day.

## 2. THE QUIET CAR

**Logline:** On the last train of the night, a woman who has been lying to her family for weeks learns that the quiet car doesn't punish noise. It punishes lies.

**Genre / tone:** Contained psychological horror. Slow-burn dread, mostly built on sound design and performance. The scares come from confession rather than gore.

**Treatment:** NOOR (40s) was laid off in September and still leaves the house every morning in her work clothes. She boards the last train in the quiet car, where three passengers sit very still: a STUDENT, an OLD MAN with a newspaper he never turns, and a NURSE in scrubs. Her husband calls, and she answers out of habit: "Just leaving the office." The lights dip and the car groans like something waking up. Noor's call has woken it. The Student scoffs and texts his mother back aloud: "At the library, Mum." The lights die, and when they return his seat is empty except for the phone, still lit. The Nurse writes on the newspaper margin: *Don't lie in here.* It's eleven minutes to the next stop. The rest of the ride is a game of small truths: the Nurse admits she hasn't slept in two days, and the Old Man reveals he has ridden this car every night since his wife died, because it's the only place nobody can pretend to him. Then Noor's phone rings again. Silence would be a lie of its own, and she knows it. She answers, and with the car listening, she tells her husband everything. The doors open. She steps onto the platform alive, phone still at her ear, her marriage now in the open. Behind her an automated voice says, *"Thank you for riding quietly."*

**Why it works as a short:** It has a single rule, established in the first three minutes and then turned on the protagonist, whose survival costs her the secret she boarded with. The rule belongs to this film because it is about honesty, not noise. Horror shorts travel well at festivals and online. The cost is moderate: one train-car set or a borrowed carriage, four actors, and heavy use of lighting cues and sound, which are cheap, instead of effects.

## 3. LOW TIDE

**Logline:** A grieving teenager drags her father onto the beach at the year's lowest tide to dig up the time capsule her late mother buried, and she has forty minutes before the sea takes back the only place it could be.

**Genre / tone:** Family drama with a ticking clock. Warm, windswept, bittersweet, with some laughs between the two leads.

**Treatment:** It's a grey morning on a wide northern beach. THEO (50s) and his daughter IRIS (16) haven't really spoken since the funeral. Iris has a hand-drawn map from her mother's notebook: paces from a rock, a line toward a lighthouse, and an X that is only reachable at the year's lowest tide. Theo calls it a waste of time, and Iris decides he never really knew her mother. They dig in the wrong place, argue, and dig again. Theo keeps correcting her pacing with oddly specific details ("She had short legs, take smaller steps"), and Iris is too angry to notice. The water starts coming back. Iris gives up, and that is when Theo finally walks the line himself and stops without counting. They hit a rusted biscuit tin at the X. Inside is one letter to Iris at sixteen, written in two alternating hands: her mother's, and Theo's. He was there. He helped bury it, and he has known where it was the whole time. He just couldn't be the one to dig it up. They read it on the dunes as the tide fills the holes they made.

**Why it works as a short:** A literal deadline gives the film momentum, and the found object recasts the relationship in one image: two handwritings on one page. It is the most emotionally accessible of the three. **Production note, to be honest about it:** two actors and one location, but not the cheapest. Shooting continuity against a real rising tide means a tight, tide-table-dependent schedule (likely two short mornings), a stand-by safety person for the water work, and minor-actor hours for Iris.
