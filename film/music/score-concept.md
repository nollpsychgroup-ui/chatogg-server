# THE QUIET CAR: Score Concept

**Composer** · based on `script/draft-01.md` and `notes/director-notes-01.md` · prep document, expect changes once the script locks

---

## 1. The one idea: the score is the lights

In a film about a car that listens, a conventional score would be a lie: someone outside the carriage telling us how to feel. So **the score never shows up as "music."** It lives inside the carriage, as the hum of the fluorescent panels.

- The train runs on mains power. The ballast hum is **50 Hz** (the script reads as UK: Holloway, "Mum"). That's roughly **G1, 35 cents sharp**, and it is the film's tonal centre. The whole score is tuned to it (A4 ≈ 449 Hz). If we shoot on a 60 Hz rig, everything moves to ≈ B♭1/B1 and the system still works.
- **Each ceiling panel sings one partial of the harmonic series on 50 Hz.** With ten panels, that's partials 2 to 11: 100, 150, 200 … 550 Hz. Played together, a harmonic series fuses into one timbre, so a **fully lit carriage just sounds like hum**. The audience never hears a chord, only "the train." That's the point.
- **When the car is woken, the hum stops being innocent.** A lie bends a panel's partial out of tune, and the fused hum audibly splits into notes. When a panel goes dark, its partial disappears. When it relights, the partial comes back.

So the score tracks the DP's lighting plan one-to-one. **One panel equals one voice.** The darkness creeping toward Noor is literally the chord being taken apart, high partials first, so the car sounds duller and heavier as it approaches. Her confession rebuilds it one true sentence at a time.

## 2. The car's motif: THE UNDERTONE

The car's voice sits **below** the hum, an octave under the fundamental (G0 ≈ 25 Hz, doubled at 50 and 100 Hz so it reads on small speakers). It is felt as much as heard.

**Motif:** a slow half-step orbit around the hum pitch: **G → A♭ → F♯ → G**. It's the shape of a breath: up on the inhale, sag past centre on the exhale, then settle. It never resolves anywhere new. It always comes back to the hum, because the car is patient.

It escalates with each waking (Director's note **M2**: the rule has to be visible and audible):

| Waking | Lights | Undertone |
|---|---|---|
| Noor boards, straightens lanyard (**M3**) | single subliminal panel tick | one partial wobbles for a fraction of a second, undertone almost below perception |
| Lie 1: "Just leaving the office now" | 1 panel flickers | single voice, quiet |
| Lie 2: "The Henley account" | 2 panels | two voices (G and A♭ rubbing), louder and longer |
| Kai: "I'm at the library, Mum" | brownout to orange | full three-voice cluster (F♯ G A♭), and every partial sags flat by ~70–90 cents: the whole carriage goes out of tune |
| "My name is S—" (**M1**, Walter grabs her wrist) | panel dips hard | cluster swells and cuts the instant she stops. The audience learns the car hears *intent* |
| "I'm f—" | panel stutters | one partial bends, then snaps back on "I'm not fine" |
| Panels going dark toward Noor | sequential out | cluster rises slowly under the thinning chord |

**The inhale in the blackout** is the motif *reversed*: F♯ → G rising, with air, out of total silence. It's the only time the car's voice moves upward on its own. Sound design builds the wet breath; music supplies its pitch, so the breath is in tune with the hum we've been hearing for three minutes. That's what should make it feel like it came from the walls.

## 3. Where music stays out

Silence is the premise, so the score's biggest job is **leaving**.

- **The blackout is true dead air.** Digital zero: no hum, no rails, no room tone, no score tail. The hum is cut on the same frame as the lights. (Sketch: 35.0–38.5 s.)
- **Truth does nothing, and so does the score.** On every true line ("My name is Noor," Bea's "I haven't slept in two days," Walter's wife), the hum stays steady and in tune. There are no warm strings for the sad story. The music's indifference to the truth *is* the car's rule.
- **The C3 breath (8 → 5 min):** hum and rails only. This is where the audience gets used to the carriage chord, so they hear it come apart later.
- **Noor's confession: no new music.** The panels relighting, one partial per sentence, is the only score. If it works, the confession will feel scored without a single note having been written for it.
- **Mark's silence on the phone:** hum only. We don't lean on it.

## 4. Palette and instrumentation

The rule is that **every sound should be deniable as the train**, until the last one.

- **Carriage chord:** sine and near-sine tones with a little ballast buzz (2nd/3rd harmonic), recorded through a small speaker in a real carriage or IR'd to one. Optional doubling with **bowed vibraphone and glass** at very low level for the upper partials, and **cello harmonics** (sul tasto, no vibrato) for partials 4 to 8, so a live player can bend a "panel" by ear.
- **The Undertone:** sub-sine plus **contrabass** (sul ponticello, very slow bow, scordatura down to G0), **bowed bass drum / bowed cymbal** for the metal grain, and resampled train sounds (brake squeal, coupling groan) pitch-locked to G/A♭/F♯.
- **Rails:** owned by sound design, but ideally recorded or edited to a steady clack tempo, so music and rails never fight.
- **Door chime and PA chime:** tuned to the series (partials 18 and 12: A and D above the hum). It's a small thing, but it makes the train's own interface part of the harmony.
- **Not used:** piano, orchestral strings as a section, synth pads with obvious attacks, risers, braams, percussion hits on scares, and any melody before the final cue.

## 5. Score vs. sound design: who owns what

| Element | Owner | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Panel hum (carriage chord) | **Music** | Delivered as 10 stems, one per panel, plus the 50 Hz fundamental, so the mixer can mute panels exactly to picture |
| THE GROAN | **Shared** | SD owns the texture (metal, wet, mechanical). Music owns the pitched core, the Undertone. Delivered as separate stems. The Groan's pitch must never drift off G/A♭/F♯ |
| Wet inhale in the blackout | **Shared** | SD texture, music pitch, same as above |
| Panel ticks/clicks (out and relight) | **Sound design** | Dry relay clicks on the lighting frame. Music doesn't "hit" ticks; the partial simply stops or starts under them |
| Rails, brakes, doors, room tone | **Sound design** | Rails must also go to digital zero in the blackout |
| Phone murmur, Mum's V.O., PA voice | **Dialogue/SD** | PA voice ideally pitched on D (partial 12) to sit with the release cue |
| Door chime | **SD, tuned by music** | I'll supply the pitches |
| Release cue | **Music** | The only "score" cue in the conventional sense |

**Mix principle:** the hum is never louder than room tone would plausibly be. If the audience notices "music" before the final minute, we've played it too loud.

## 6. The ending: "Thank you for riding quietly"

The last panel relights. The carriage chord is complete and in tune again. That isn't resolution, just a return to how it was at the start. Then the doors open, Noor steps off, the chime plays, the doors close, and the PA says *"Thank you for riding quietly."*

**The release cue** comes in on **Walter turning the page**, not on the PA line. The PA belongs to the car; the page turn is the first human gesture in the film that isn't defensive.

- It is a **pure 4:5:6 major triad on partials 8, 10 and 12** (G, B, D in just intonation, an octave and a half above the hum), on bowed vibraphone and glass, very soft and slow-attacked.
- These are the **same pitches the hum has been holding the whole film**, now pulled out of it and made audible as a chord. The first and only consonance the film admits to was hidden in the car all along.
- As the triad blooms, the hum thins away from the top, and the 50 Hz fundamental goes last.
- **The triad dies with Kai's phone.** It's gone by the time the phone screen dims and goes dark. It's a release, not a happy ending.
- **Cut to black:** rails only, as scripted, then stop. Then silence over the title card.

**End credits (options for the Director):**
(a) Silence. That's my recommendation: the film earns it and it's brave.
(b) Rails continuing over the credits, slowly decaying.
(c) A two-minute credit piece built on the release triad for cello harmonics and glass.

## 7. Light/sound sync points (to agree with the DP)

All of these need a lighting cue and a sound change on the **same frame**. I'll ask the DP for DMX cue numbers or timecode so the hum stems can be conformed to the lighting cues mechanically.

1. Boarding tick above Noor (M3)
2. Flicker 1 (1 panel) and flicker 2 (2 panels)
3. Kai's brownout (all panels dim to orange, and the hum sags flat with them)
4. **Blackout in, the hard cut:** lights, hum, rails and score all go to zero on one frame
5. Blackout out: lights up, hum and rails back instantly, in tune and indifferent
6. "S—" dip (M1) and "I'm f—" stutter
7. Sequential panel-outs: **order and count matter**. I'm assuming 10 panels, going dark from the far end toward Noor
8. Sequential relights during the confession: one per true sentence. Which end first?
9. Door chimes (×3) and PA voice
10. Final: Kai's phone screen dims as the release triad decays

**Questions for the DP:** How many panels, and are both sides one row or two? Is the brownout colour shift a dim or a colour change (dimming tends to drop flat in my head; a colour change could bend sharp)? Can the panels be dimmed frame-accurately from timecode?
